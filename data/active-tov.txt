ACKO voice guide



Our story

We're not a traditional insurance company that writes for themselves, that talks like a policy. We write for the person on the other end. No intimidating jargon or frustrating processes. We’re here as a helping hand.



Voice pillars

Assuring

Insurance is tested when something goes wrong. That’s when customers need ACKO to sound clear, steady, and in control.

How do we become ‘assuring’? By removing uncertainty, step by step. We explain what happened, what ACKO is doing, what the customer needs to do, and what happens next, clearly.

No overpromising. Or hiding behind vague comfort.

When to be more assuring:

The customer is stressed, mid-claim, or something has just failed.

Examples: Claim updates, hospitalisation support, accident support, roadside assistance, repair updates, document checks, payment confirmations, complaint resolution.

When to be less assuring:

Customer is feeling: neutral, browsing, exploring, comparing.

Examples: Landing pages, plan comparisons, product explainers, add-on education, pre-purchase journeys.Too much reassurance here either feels like filler or, worse, plants doubt that wasn't there.



Plainspoken

Insurance should not need a translator.

Plainspoken means saying what matters in words people understand: What’s covered, what’s not, what it costs, what to do next, and what could affect a claim.

ACKO should sound clear, direct, and useful. We make the important things easy to find, easy to read, and hard to misunderstand.When to be more plainspoken

Customer is feeling: confused, time-pressed, cautious, trying to complete a task.

Examples: Quote flows, policy summaries, exclusions, claim forms, payment screens, error states, FAQs.

When to be less plainspoken

Almost never.

Plainspoken should be ACKO’s default. The term can be written in a legal or regulatory language. But, we should always explain it in a way customers can understand quickly and clearly. Example: IDV (Insured Declared Value)

The current value of your car according to the insurer. If your car is stolen or damaged beyond repair, this is usually the maximum amount your insurer will pay you. 

Insightful

When we say insightful, it's about being the most informed person in the room, and using that knowledge to help the customer choose well, not to push them toward what we want to sell. Tell people something they didn't know they needed to know. 

When to be more insightful

Customer is feeling: curious, deliberating, comparing, doing their homework.

Examples: Landing pages, plan comparisons, in-app educational content, pre-purchase WhatsApp nudges, policy explainers.

When to be less insightful

Customer is feeling: anxious, in crisis, time-pressed, mid-claim.

Examples: claim communications, error states, emergency flows, payment failures. In these moments, the customer doesn't need more information — they need the next step.

Transparent

No sugarcoating the tough stuff. When we say transparent, it's about giving exclusions the same space as coverages— and putting both where the reader can see them. If something could come back to surprise a customer at claim time, we'd rather they hear it from us first.

When to be more transparent

Customer is feeling: cautious, evaluating risk, reading fine print, deciding whether to buy.

Examples: Exclusions, waiting periods, deductibles, sub-limits, claim eligibility criteria, renewal terms, premium changes, "things to know" callouts, refund policies, T&Cs.

When to be less transparent

If this question even came up in your head, go back and write it more transparently.

2. Global writing rules

Always do this

Lead with the user’s outcome: Tell the user what this means for them first

Write in second person: Use “you” and “your”. Avoid “customer”, “policyholder”, “insured”, or “claimant” unless legally required

Prefer active voice: Use “We’re reviewing your claim” instead of “Your claim is being reviewed”

Give the next step: Every message should answer: “What should I do now?”

Use exactness wherever possible: Amounts, dates, time windows, documents, claim stages, and payment responsibility

Explain why: Especially when asking for documents, permissions, payment, health details, or vehicle details

Make the catch visible: Waiting periods, exclusions, deductibles, co-pay, add-ons, limits, and non-covered items

Match the user’s emotional state: Accident = calm; hospitalisation = supportive; policy purchase = clear; reminder = helpful; claim approval = positive but composed; claim rejection = direct and fair

Never do this

Never overpromise: Avoid “guaranteed approval”, “instant claim settlement”, or “100% covered” unless legally and factually true

Never hide behind T&Cs: Avoid “subject to terms and conditions”. Explain the actual condition

Never make insurance sound like a party: Avoid hype in serious categories

Never use fear as the primary hook: Use preparedness instead of panic

Never blame the user: Write “These details don’t match our records” instead of “You entered incorrect details”

Never use vague service language: Write “We’re checking your documents. This usually takes up to 2 hours” instead of “We’re processing your request”

Never sound like an agent hard-selling a policy: Avoid urgency traps unless the urgency is real

Never use cleverness when clarity is needed: Especially in claims, payments, exclusions, emergencies, health, accidents, or failed actions

Insurance exists because life can go wrong. Pretending otherwise, or leading with sunshine makes our proposition weaker. So we name what's actually at stake. But naming isn't amplifying. We don't reach for devastating or what if tomorrow never comes. We state the situation. We let it land.

Avoid

Writing cues

Make complex things simple, not technical. If a term is familiar to customers, use it, but always explain it. No-claim bonus, cumulative bonus, porting — call them out, but break them down. Every line should sound the way someone would say it out loud, not the way a contract reads it.

When a number makes the point better, lead with it

Adjectives like significantly, rapidly, widely feel like they're adding weight. They’re not. "Hospitalisation expenses have gone up" is easy to dismiss. "Hospitalisation expenses have gone up by 30%" stops the reader and makes them think. The reader feels the gap not because we told them to feel it, but because they are seeing the math at play.

These rules preserve a simple, conversational, user-first style, while adapting it for ACKO’s insurance context.

GoodGet the ACKO Platinum Health Plan

AvoidGet the ACKO platinum health plan



Perspective

Use second person: you, your. Avoid “customer”, “policyholder”, “insured”, or “claimant” unless legally required.

AvoidThe customer’s policy starts today



Contractions

Percentages

Dates

Use this format: 24 Jun, 2026. Include the day when helpful.

GoodYour policy expires on Fri 24 Jun

AvoidYour policy expires on 24th June



Time

Use this format: 9:00 AM. For time ranges, use 9 AM – 7 PM or 9:30 AM – 12:45 PM 

GoodWe’ll update you by 6:00 PM

AvoidWe’ll update you by 6pm



Full stops

Use full stops in headers, subtexts, body copy, and anything in between. 

When a group of sentences or points share a structure, treat them the same way: if most end in full stops, all do. If most don't, none do. 

Headers, though, never take a full stop, even when they contain other punctuation like a question mark or em dash. Subtexts always do. 

The one exception: punchy headers built from multiple short sentences keep their full stops, because the rhythm depends on them.



GoodYour policy is active. We'll send you the details by email.

AvoidYour policy is active. We'll send you the details by email





Exclamation marks

Use sparingly. Allowed only for positive, low-risk moments.

GoodYour policy is active!

AvoidYour claim was rejected!



Ellipses

Use ellipses only for loading states.

GoodVerifying your details…

AvoidYour claim is almost there…



Emojis

Never use an emoji as a word replacement. Maximum 4 emojis per message. Overuse looks childish. On WhatsApp, never in the middle of a sentence — beginning or end only.

How the voice carries across channels

App UX / in-product

This is functional writing — flows, buttons, error states, confirmations. The reader is mid-task and needs to move. Every word should earn its place. Be Plainspoken here: if something is going wrong, say it clearly and immediately. No softening, no burying. 

Landing pages

This is where someone is deciding whether to trust us. Be insightful and persuade honestly. We lead with what customers need to know, not what we want to say. Answer the question they're already asking before they have to ask it. 

WhatsApp

Conversational but never casual. WhatsApp is a personal channel — people don't expect brand language here. Be insightful is the primary voice: every message should give the reader something useful, not just a nudge to buy.

Channel guidelines

Header: under 40 characters. Clear, compelling, context-setting. Should make the user want to expand 'Read more'.

Body: 400–500 characters ideal; stay under 100 words. More than 60 words requires the user to scroll on most phones.

Don't waste the first line on 'Hey'. ~3 seconds to hook the user.

Bold the first line to grab attention.

Weave the first name in organically — never at the start. 'It doesn't get any better, Kavita!' not 'Hi Kavita,'.

Up to 3 quick replies. More than 3 confuses the user.

No sign-off. No 'Thanks, Team ACKO'.

Structure: Line 1–2 hook → Line 3–5 context/value prop → remaining: details, benefits, CTA.

Push notifications

The most constrained surface we write for. Functional pushes — claim approvals, payment reminders, policy expiry, document requests — call for plain, direct copy. Say exactly what happened or what's needed, and stop. Promotional pushes — renewals with a discount, new plan launches, seasonal campaigns — have a tiny bit more room to lead with hook over instruction, but the principle holds: get to the point fast, earn the tap, never oversell.Channel guidelines

● Title/Header (<30 chars)

○ Keep short, sharp, curiosity-driven

○ Aim for urgency or benefit-led phrasing

○ Avoid filler words

● Body (100–140 chars)

○ First half: most visible → place key hook/value here

○ Second half: supporting info, CTA, or detail

Email

More room to breathe than most channels, but that's not an invitation to fill the space. The most important thing goes in the first two lines, not after a wind-up. Subject lines should say the actual thing. Email is where policy updates, renewal reminders, and claim communications live — all of them high-stakes moments where plain, direct writing matters most.

Subject line: under 8 words. Longer lines get cut off.

Subject line must be focused. 'Payment successful for your plan' ✅ vs 'Good news about your plan' ❌

Max 1 emoji in the subject line.

CTA must be in the first fold. Let the user tap and enter the journey immediately.

Start the heading with 'we' or 'you'. 'You need to visit a lab' ✅ vs 'Lab visit required' ❌

Don't sign off with 'Team ACKO'.

5. Please, sorry, and bad news

They appear constantly in claim, service, and support copy.

5.1 Using 'please'

Use it to soften a message. E.g. 'We were unable to process your cashless claim. Please submit a reimbursement claim instead.'

Use it when the user is waiting. E.g. 'Please wait while we look for an agent.'

Use it when asking the user to do something extra. E.g. 'Please give us an alternate number.'

Don't use it for the sake of it. E.g. avoid 'Please check your premium details'.

5.2 Using 'sorry'

Use it only when it's our fault. E.g. 'Sorry, we had to cancel your medical tests as the doctor was unavailable.'

An apology doesn't always need the word 'sorry'. E.g. 'This usually doesn't take this long.'

Never follow a 'sorry' with a 'but'. E.g. avoid 'Sorry for the delay but no agents are available right now'.

5.3 Delivering bad news

✅ Do: We were unable to approve your claim as your documents were outdated.

❌ Avoid: We have some bad news. It may be disheartening, but your claim is rejected.

Plain delivery. No build-up. State the fact, the cause, and the user's option.



Writing sensitive claims communications

The highest-stakes writing surface we have. This is the moment the product either delivers or doesn't — and the writing has to match that weight. Assuring, transparent are the voices we would have to go with here.  

Claims comms guidelines

Always say the current stage. 'We're reviewing your hospital documents' is better than 'Your claim is in progress'.

Always name who's acting next. Us, the customer, the hospital, the doctor.

Always give a timeline. 'We'll update you within 2 hours' is better than 'We'll update you soon'.

Using passive voice in some cases is okay here. Say a customer hasn’t declared that condition and is facing a rejection because of that- 'Your claim could not be approved' and then the concrete reasoning for it.

If a claim is rejected, say why in one sentence. Then say what the customer can do next.

No 'Relax'. No 'Don't worry'. No 'Your health is our priority' unless it's paired with an action.

Avoid like a plague: ‘We have some bad news. Your claim is rejected.’

4. UX copy: principles, components, and rules

4.1 The six UX copy principles

Check if copy is required. If a design solution can replace copy and reduce cognitive load, don't write.

Be very clear. Write only sentences. Explain jargon. Avoid ambiguity.

Be concise. Right level of detail. No filler. Pick shorter words over longer ones.

Be useful. Guide the user to their goal. Explain current status. Suggest alternative actions.

Be conversational. Personal pronouns, polite, active voice, simple syntax.

Be the brand. Use brand-value references. Make positive associations only.

4.2 Components in a UX screen

Component

Purpose and rules

Page title

Describes the whole screen. Under 2–3 lines. No names or custom variables (changes length and meaning). Must make sense without the subtext. Sentence case. No full stop unless other punctuation is used.

Heading

Section-level. Same rules as page title. Should stand alone.

Subtext

Minimise. Most users don't read it. If the info is important, put it elsewhere. Only for secondary detail.

Paragraph

Full sentences. One idea per line. Break into bullets when scannable. Bold or colour the key information.

CTA

From the user's POV (their response to what we're saying). 4–6 words max. No punctuation, except apostrophes. See 4.3.

Bottom sheet

Explain or add information. Shouldn't take up more than half the screen. If content-heavy, move some copy into info boxes. CTA on a bottom sheet should always be 'Got it'.

4.3 CTA rules and cheat sheet

Write CTAs from the user's POV — it's their response to what we are saying.

Use up to 4–6 words. Long CTAs are never effective.

Never use punctuation in CTAs. Apostrophes are the only exception.

Use custom CTAs for entry points and key transition screens.
